How Your Paycheck Is Calculated in Every State (2026 Guide)

You accepted the job at $75,000. Then the first paycheck arrived, and it was... less. A lot less. Where did the money go? This guide walks through every line of a US paycheck — federal tax, FICA, state tax, and deductions — and explains why the same salary buys a very different lifestyle in Texas versus California.

The anatomy of a paycheck

Every paycheck is your gross pay minus four layers:

  1. 1. Federal income tax — the biggest bite for most people. The US uses marginal brackets: in 2026, the first chunk of your income is taxed at 10%, the next at 12%, then 22%, and so on. A common myth: "a raise pushed me into a higher bracket so I take home less." Never true — only the dollars above the bracket line are taxed at the higher rate.
  1. 2. FICA (Social Security + Medicare) — 7.65% on most wages: 6.2% Social Security (up to the annual wage cap) and 1.45% Medicare (no cap, plus an extra 0.9% on high earners). Your employer matches this, so the true cost of employing you is higher than your salary.
  1. 3. State income tax — the layer that varies most. Nine states have no income tax at all. Others range from flat rates to graduated brackets topping 10%+.
  1. 4. Pre-tax and post-tax deductions — health insurance premiums, 401(k) contributions, HSA/FSA, dental and vision. Pre-tax deductions lower your taxable income; a $200 monthly 401(k) contribution doesn't just save for retirement, it shrinks this year's tax bill.

Same salary, different state: a real example

Take a single filer earning $75,000 with no special deductions. In Texas (no state income tax), take-home is roughly $57,000–$58,000 a year. In California, state tax takes another ~$4,000+, leaving roughly $53,000. That's a $4,000+ annual gap from the state line alone — before cost of living even enters the picture.

Now add the 2026 twist: the federal standard deduction and brackets are inflation-adjusted each year, so last year's calculator is wrong this year. Always use current-year figures.

The 5 inputs that change everything

A paycheck calculator is only as good as its inputs: (1) pay frequency — biweekly vs semi-monthly changes each check's size; (2) filing status — single vs married filing jointly shifts every bracket; (3) pre-tax deductions — 401(k), HSA, and premiums come off before tax; (4) additional withholding — the extra amount from Step 4(c) of your W-4; (5) state and locality — some cities (like New York City) add their own income tax on top.

How to read your pay stub in 30 seconds

Find three numbers: gross pay (what you earned), total deductions (what left before you saw it), net pay (what hit your account). If net pay changed from last month and your salary didn't, the cause is almost always a deduction change — benefits open enrollment, a 401(k) rate change, or a W-4 update.

One thing to do this week

Run your own numbers through a current-year calculator with your actual W-4 settings and deductions. Then compare your state against a no-income-tax state — not necessarily to move, but to understand exactly what your state costs you. Knowledge first, decisions second.
